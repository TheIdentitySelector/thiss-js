The Triaxial Shape and Surface Irregularities of Mars: Geological Processes and the Nerio Hypothesis

Tianyi Hao; Alejandro Sánchez-Postigo; Pavel Cheben; Alejandro Ortega-Moñux;

Abstract

Mars differs significantly from a perfect sphere and is more accurately described as a triaxial ellipsoid with large-scale surface irregularities. Unlike Earth, whose stronger gravity smooths much of its surface into a more uniform shape, Mars displays substantial topographic variation caused by volcanic activity, crustal thickness differences, ancient impacts, and tectonic processes. These irregularities produce a planetary form often compared to a lumpy or imbalanced rugby ball.

In addition to established geological explanations, some theoretical models propose that Mars may once have possessed an ancient moon, sometimes referred to hypothetically as Nerio, whose gravitational influence and eventual destruction could have contributed to crustal deformation and planetary imbalance.

Introduction

Large celestial bodies usually become spherical because gravity pulls material inward equally from all directions. However, Mars differs from a nearly perfect sphere because of its geological history and uneven internal structure. The planet is better described as a triaxial ellipsoid, meaning its three main axes are slightly different in length.

Mars contains some of the largest geological formations in the Solar System, including Olympus Mons, the tallest known volcano, and the Tharsis volcanic plateau. The planet also has a strong hemispheric difference, where the southern hemisphere is older and heavily cratered while the northern hemisphere is smoother and lower in elevation.

Some theories additionally propose that Mars may once have had a large moon called Nerio. According to this idea, tidal forces from the moon may have affected the Martian crust before the moon eventually broke apart or collided with the planet.

Methodology

This article uses theoretical and observational analysis based on planetary science and astrophysics. Data from Mars orbiters and surface mapping missions are used to examine Martian topography, crustal thickness, and gravitational variation. Comparisons are also made between Mars and other terrestrial planets in order to understand how gravity, rotation, and geological activity influence planetary shape.

Discussion

The irregular shape of Mars is largely caused by its relatively weak gravity. Mars has much less mass than Earth, meaning gravity is less effective at smoothing large surface structures over time. Rotational forces also contribute to the planet’s triaxial shape; as Mars rotates, centrifugal forces create slight expansion near the equator. However, the greatest distortions come from uneven mass distribution across the surface and interior.

The Tharsis volcanic region is one of the largest volcanic structures in the Solar System and strongly affects the planet’s balance. Olympus Mons alone rises about 22 kilometers above the surrounding terrain. Because Mars lacks active plate tectonics, these volcanic structures remain preserved for billions of years.

The speculative Nerio hypothesis suggests that an ancient Martian moon may have produced tidal stresses on the crust, contributing to geological instability and asymmetry. Although there is no direct evidence for Nerio, tidal interactions are known to affect planetary bodies elsewhere in the Solar System.

Conclusion

Mars is shaped like a triaxial ellipsoid because of the combined effects of gravity, rotation, volcanic activity, crustal asymmetry, and ancient impacts. Unlike Earth, Mars has weaker gravity and limited tectonic activity, allowing large irregular structures to remain preserved.

The study of Mars demonstrates that planetary shapes are influenced not only by gravity, but also by geological history, internal dynamics, and external interactions over billions of years.
